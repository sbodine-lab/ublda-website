# Research notes for plain-spoken copy

These findings help identify editing candidates. They do not prove that an individual passage was written by AI, and most measured academic or biomedical prose rather than club websites.

- [Kobak et al., *Science Advances* (2025)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12219543/) compared more than 15 million PubMed abstracts from 2010–2024. Style words including *delve* and *underscore* rose sharply after 2022. The authors measure corpus-level shifts, not a detector for individual pages.
- [Matsui, *Perspectives on Medical Education* (2025)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12679996/) tracked 135 terms associated with LLM writing in PubMed records. Among the largest increases were *delve*, *underscore*, *meticulous*, *boast*, *showcase*, *intricate*, *tapestry*, and *unlocking*. Some terms were already rising before ChatGPT, so avoid treating them as exclusive AI markers.
- [Why Does ChatGPT “Delve” So Much?, COLING (2025)](https://aclanthology.org/2025.coling-main.426.pdf) examined overrepresented words in ChatGPT rewrites of scientific abstracts and possible sources of that preference. Its context is scientific writing; use the vocabulary findings as a prompt to edit, not a universal ban.
- [Wikipedia editors' field guide to signs of AI writing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Signs_of_AI_writing) documents observable patterns such as generic promotional language, formulaic phrasing, and repeated structure. It explicitly notes that no single sign proves AI authorship. Its examples are editorial observations, not a controlled frequency study.

The advice on repeated short slogans and commas also comes from the requesting editor's examples and from reviewing the target pages. Those patterns are a house-style concern here, not a claim that research uniquely identifies them as AI-generated.
